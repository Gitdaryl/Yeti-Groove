What the day actually looks like.

We spend a day inside how your business really runs, which is never quite how the software says it runs. We follow one job from the first phone call to the final invoice and write down every place a human retypes, re-sends, or remembers something.

Then you get a short document in plain language: what is costing you hours, what can be automated this month, and what should be left alone. That last part matters. Plenty of what you do by hand is judgement, and judgement should stay with you.

The part most people skip

Anything we leave running, we watch. Automations break quietly. A password expires, a service changes a rule, and a thing that has worked for eight months stops without telling anybody. So every system we install comes with a monthly care fee and a health check that shouts when something goes wrong.

We do not hand over unattended machinery and walk away. That is how businesses end up worse off than before they automated anything.

We are not a tech company. We make things.

Good. The businesses that gain the most from this are the least technical ones, because nobody has ever looked at their back office with fresh eyes. Fabricators, contractors, marinas, restaurants. If you can walk us through a job, we can find the leaks.

Are you going to tell us to replace our software?

Almost certainly not. Ripping out a system your people already know is expensive and it is usually the wrong answer. Most of what we find lives in the gaps between the tools you already pay for.

Is this an AI thing?

Some of it, where it earns its place. Most of it is much more boring than that: connecting two things that should already have been connected. The goal is fewer hours, not more technology.
